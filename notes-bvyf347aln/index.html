Lesson 1

Chess notation

Notation is how we write moves down, so we can replay our games, share them and learn from them. Every chess player in the world uses the same system.

Squares

Columns are files, lettered a to h from White's left. Rows are ranks, numbered 1 to 8 from White's side. Every square has a name: file first, then rank, so e4, d5, h7.

Pawn captures

A pawn capture starts with the file the pawn came from. exd5 means "the e-pawn captures on d5". If White has pawns on c4 and e4 and both can take on d5, writing cxd5 or exd5 tells us exactly which one did.

When two pieces can go to the same square

Add the piece's file (or rank) so the move can only mean one thing. With knights on b1 and f3, both can reach d2: write Nbd2 or Nfd2. With rooks on e1 and e8, both can reach e4: write R1e4 or R8e4.

Comment symbols and results

  • ! good move · !! brilliant
  • ? mistake · ?? blunder
  • !? interesting · ?! dubious
  • 1-0 White wins
  • 0-1 Black wins
  • ½-½ draw
Tournament rule

Play your move on the board first, then write it on your scoresheet. Writing a move down before you play it isn't allowed under FIDE rules.

Practice: write it out

This position comes from 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 a6 4. Ba4 Nf6 5. O-O Be7. Set up a board and play it through, saying each move out loud. Then write these in notation:

  1. White's rook goes from f1 to e1.
  2. Black's b-pawn moves two squares.
  3. White's bishop goes back from a4 to b3.
Show answer

1. Re1   2. b5   3. Bb3. These are the next moves of the main line of the Ruy Lopez (Spanish Opening): 6. Re1 b5 7. Bb3.

Lesson 2

Controlling the centre

The four centre squares are d4, e4, d5 and e5. Pieces in the centre reach more squares, attack and defend better, and can swing to either side of the board quickly.

Three ways to fight for the centre

  • Occupy it with pawns. 1. e4 and 1. d4 put a pawn in the centre and open lines for the bishops and queen.
  • Support it with pieces. Knights on f3 and c3 (f6 and c6 for Black) hit d4, e4, d5 and e5 together. A knight on the edge controls far fewer squares, which is why "a knight on the rim is dim".
  • Control it from a distance. A fianchettoed bishop on g2 or b2 aims straight down the long diagonal at the centre. This is the "hypermodern" idea used in openings like the King's Indian and the Réti.

Gambits: giving a pawn to win the centre

Queen's Gambit: 1. d4 d5 2. c4

White offers the c-pawn to pull Black's d5 pawn away from the centre, so White can follow up with e4. It isn't a true sacrifice, because White can usually win the pawn back.

King's Gambit: 1. e4 e5 2. f4

White offers the f-pawn to pull Black's e5 pawn away, so White can play d4 and use the open f-file. This one is a real gambit: White often stays a pawn down for the attack.

Opening checklist

  • Put a pawn or two in the centre.
  • Develop knights and bishops towards the centre. Knights usually come out before bishops.
  • Castle early to keep your king safe.
  • Try not to move the same piece twice before your others are out.
  • Don't bring your queen out early, or she gets chased around and you lose time.
  • Connect your rooks: when nothing is left between them on the back rank, development is done.
Lesson 3

Pawn play

Pawns shape the battlefield: they decide where your pieces can go. Pawns can't move backwards, so every pawn move is permanent. Think before you push!

Pawn structures to know

Pawn chain
Pawns protecting each other on a diagonal (for example d4–e5). The back pawn is the base, the front pawn is the head.
Pawn islands
Groups of pawns separated by empty files. Fewer islands are easier to defend.
Isolated pawn
No friendly pawns on the files next to it. It gives your pieces open lines, but it can't be protected by a pawn, so it can become a target.
Doubled pawns
Two pawns on the same file. Usually a weakness, but sometimes worth it for an open file.
Backward pawn
A pawn that has fallen behind its neighbours and can't safely advance. The square in front of it is often a great outpost for the opponent.
Passed pawn
No enemy pawns in front of it on its own file or the files next to it. Passed pawns win endgames.
Pawn majority
More pawns than your opponent on one side of the board, for example 3 against 2 on the queenside. Advance the majority to create a passed pawn.

Open and closed positions

Open: centre pawns have been exchanged. Bishops and rooks love open lines, and tactics come quickly.

Closed: centre pawns are locked together. Knights get stronger, plans are slower, and the game is decided by pawn breaks.

Pawn breaks: attack the base

A pawn break is a pawn move that challenges an enemy pawn and opens lines. Against a pawn chain, the strongest plan is usually to attack its base, because the whole chain depends on it.

Example: White's chain is d4–e5 and Black's is d5–e6 (a French Defence structure).

  • Black attacks White's base with ...c5 and White's head with ...f6.
  • White attacks Black's base (e6) with f4–f5, and Black's head (d5) with c4.

Pawns and bishops

  • Put your pawns on the opposite colour to your own bishop, so the bishop has open diagonals.
  • Try to fix your opponent's pawns on the same colour as their bishop. Then the bishop is blocked by its own pawns, and those pawns become targets for you.

Attacking and defending with pawns

  • When kings have castled on opposite sides, storm the enemy king with your pawns. Pawns lead, pieces follow.
  • When kings have castled on the same side, pushing pawns in front of your own king creates holes. Attack in the centre or on the other wing instead.
  • When your opponent attacks on a wing, the classic answer is to strike back in the centre.

Puzzle: the breakthrough

White to move. The kings are too far away to help. How can White's pawns force a new queen?

Show answer

1. b6! If 1...axb6 2. c6! bxc6 3. a6 and the a-pawn queens. If 1...cxb6 2. a6! bxa6 3. c6 and the c-pawn queens. Sacrificing two pawns opens a path for the third.

If Black were to move first, 1...b6 would stop the trick, so the breakthrough only works when it's your move.

Practice game

Play a "pawn war" with a friend: 8 pawns and a king each, no other pieces. First to make a queen wins. It's the best way to learn breaks, races and passed pawns.

Lesson 4

Forks

A fork is one piece attacking two or more enemy pieces at the same time. Your opponent can only move one piece per turn, so something is usually lost.

  • Every piece can fork: knights, bishops, rooks, queens, pawns and even the king.
  • Knights are the best forkers because they jump and attack in a pattern that's easy to miss. A knight fork of king and queen is called a "royal fork".
  • A rook forks pieces along its rank and its file. One target can be on the rank and the other on the file.
  • A pawn fork is cheap and deadly: you're happy to give a pawn for any piece.

How to escape a fork

  • Capture the forking piece.
  • Move one attacked piece to safety while it defends the other, or makes a threat of its own.
  • Block, if the fork comes from a bishop, rook or queen.
  • Counter-attack with a bigger threat, such as a check.
Coach's tip

Look for enemy pieces that a knight could attack together. If the king and the queen are a knight's move apart from the same square, look for a way to get your knight there.

Puzzle 1

White to move and win the queen.

Show answer

1. Ne7+! A royal fork: the knight checks the king and attacks the queen. After the king moves, 2. Nxc6.

Puzzle 2

Black to move. Black is a knight down after 4...Nxe4 5. Nxe4. How does Black win it back?

Show answer

5...d5! The pawn forks the bishop on c4 and the knight on e4. Black wins one of them back and the material is level. This is called the "fork trick".

Lesson 5

Skewers

A skewer attacks a valuable piece that has a less valuable piece behind it on the same line. The front piece has to move, and the piece behind is captured.

In frontBehind
Pinsmaller piecebigger piece
Skewerbigger piecesmaller piece

Only pieces that move in straight lines can skewer: bishops, rooks and queens. The most common skewer is a check, because the king must move.

Defending

Don't leave your king on the same line as an unprotected piece. If you are skewered, look for a king move that protects the piece behind.

Puzzle 1

White to move and win the queen.

Show answer

1. Ra6+! The king is in front and the queen is behind on the 6th rank. The king has to step off the rank, then 2. Rxh6.

Puzzle 2

White to move. Find the skewer.

Show answer

1. Bf3+! The bishop checks the king along the long diagonal, and the rook on a8 is behind it. The king moves and 2. Bxa8 wins the rook.

Lesson 6

Pins

A pin is when a piece can't move (or really shouldn't) because a more valuable piece is behind it. Pins freeze your opponent's pieces in place.

Two types of pin

Absolute pin: the king is behind
Moving the pinned piece would put its own king in check, so it is illegal to move it off the line. (A pinned rook, bishop or queen can still slide along the line of the pin, and can even capture the pinning piece if it moves that way.)
Relative pin: something else valuable is behind
Moving the pinned piece is legal, but it would lose the queen or a rook. Relative pins can be broken if moving the piece creates a bigger threat. See the Elephant Trap puzzle below.

Only bishops, rooks and queens can pin. Knights, pawns and kings can't, because they don't attack along lines.

How to use a pin

  • Attack the pinned piece again, especially with a pawn. A pinned piece can't run away.
  • Pile up: add more attackers than it has defenders.
  • Don't forget it still bites. A piece pinned to its king still gives check and still guards squares around the enemy king.
  • Pins are often the start of a bigger combination: forks, discovered attacks and mates.

How to break a pin

  • Move the king (or the valuable piece) off the line.
  • Put another piece in between.
  • Attack the pinning piece, often with a pawn ("put the question to the bishop").
  • Exchange the pinning piece.
  • Counter-attack with something bigger.

Common pins

  • Bishop pinning a knight to the king: the most common pin of all.
  • Bishop pinning a knight to the queen, like Bg5 against ...Nf6 with the queen on d8.
  • Rook pinning a piece to the king on an open file.
  • Pinning a pawn, so it can no longer defend or capture.

Puzzle 1

White to move. The bishop on e6 is pinned. How do you win it?

Show answer

1. d5! Attack the pinned piece with a pawn. The bishop can't move off the e-file without exposing the king, so 2. dxe6 wins it.

Puzzle 2: The Elephant Trap

Black to move. White has just taken on d5, thinking the knight on f6 is pinned to the queen. Is it?

Show answer

6...Nxd5! The pin is only relative, so Black can break it. After 7. Bxd8 Bb4+! 8. Qd2 Bxd2+ 9. Kxd2 Kxd8 Black has won a piece.

Puzzle 3: Légal's Mate

White to move. The knight on f3 is pinned to the queen. Can White ignore the pin?

Show answer

5. Nxe5! If Black grabs the queen with 5...Bxd1?? then 6. Bxf7+ Ke7 7. Nd5#. Black's best is 5...dxe5 6. Qxg4 and White is a pawn up.

Lesson 7

Discovered attacks, discovered checks and double check

A discovered attack happens when one piece moves out of the way and reveals an attack from a bishop, rook or queen behind it, like pulling back a curtain.

  • Two threats in one move: the revealed attack is one threat, and the piece that moved can make a second.
  • Discovered check: the revealed attack is a check. Your opponent has to deal with the check, so the piece that moved can grab almost anything.
  • Double check: the piece that moves also gives check. You can't block two checks or capture two pieces at once, so the king must move.
  • The windmill: a repeating discovered check, where the moving piece captures something every time. The most famous one is Torre v Lasker, Moscow 1925.
Coach's tip

Whenever one of your pieces stands between your rook, bishop or queen and an enemy piece, ask: "Where can the front piece go with a threat of its own?"

Puzzle 1

White to move and win the queen.

Show answer

1. Bxa8+ The bishop takes the queen and uncovers a check from the rook on e1. Black is too busy with the check to recapture.

Puzzle 2: Réti v Tartakower, Vienna 1910

White to move and mate in 3. Your queen is attacked, but don't move her to safety!

Show answer

9. Qd8+!! Kxd8 10. Bg5+ is a double check, from the bishop and the rook on d1. After 10...Kc7 11. Bd8# (or 10...Ke8 11. Rd8#).

Lesson 8

Double attacks

A double attack is any move that creates two threats at once. If your opponent can only answer one, you win the other.

  • A fork is a double attack by one piece. All forks are double attacks, but not all double attacks are forks.
  • Two pieces can share the work, for example when a discovered attack reveals one threat and the moving piece makes another.
  • The threats don't have to be captures. "Check plus attack a piece" and "threaten mate plus attack a piece" are both double attacks.
Loose pieces drop off

Most double attacks hit an unprotected piece. Before every move, check which of your pieces are undefended.

Puzzle

White to move. Black's rook on a5 isn't protected. Find a queen move that checks the king and attacks the rook.

Show answer

1. Qd8+ checks along the back rank and attacks the rook along the d8–a5 diagonal. After 1...Kf7 2. Qxa5. (1. Qb3+ followed by Qb4+ also works.)

Lesson 9

The tactics toolbox

A tactic is a short, forcing idea, usually one to three moves, that wins material or gives mate. Here are all the tactics we use, each with how to defend against it.

Removing the defender
Capture or chase away the piece guarding something important. Defend: add a second defender.
Deflection
Force a defender to leave its post. Defend: don't give pieces two jobs.
Decoy (attraction)
Lure an enemy piece, often the king, onto a bad square, usually with a sacrifice. Defend: ask why the material is being offered.
Overloading
One piece has too many jobs. Attack one of them and the other falls. Defend: share the jobs out.
X-ray
A piece attacks or defends through another piece on the same line. Defend: count attackers on the whole line.
Battery
Two pieces lined up on the same line, like queen and bishop aimed at h7, or doubled rooks. Defend: block or exchange the front piece.
Interference
Put a piece between an enemy defender and what it defends. Defend: capture the blocking piece.
Clearance
Move a piece out of the way, often with a threat, to open a square or line for another piece. Defend: look at what the move opens up.
Zwischenzug (in-between move)
Instead of the expected recapture, play a stronger move first, usually a check or bigger threat. Defend: check every forcing move before you capture.
Trapped piece
A piece with no safe squares. Bishops on a7 or h7 and knights on the edge are classic victims. Defend: keep an escape square.
Back-rank weakness
A king boxed in by its own pawns can be mated by a rook or queen. Defend: make "luft" (an escape square) with h3 or g3.
Quiet move
A non-checking move that sets up an unstoppable threat. Defend: after every move, ask "what does it threaten?"
Zugzwang
Any move makes your position worse, but you have to move. It mostly happens in endgames. Defend: keep spare moves, like pawn moves.
Perpetual check
Endless checks that force a draw. A great escape when you're losing. Defend: find a shelter for your king.
Combinations

Strong players join tactics together into a combination, like a mini story: sacrifice, check, fork, win. When one tactic doesn't quite work, ask whether a second one can finish the job.

Puzzle: remove the defender

White to move. White's queen and bishop make a battery aimed at h7, but the knight on f6 guards that square. What should White do?

Show answer

1. Bxf6! removes the defender. If Black recaptures with 1...Qxf6 or 1...gxf6, then 2. Qxh7#. Black's only way to stop mate is 1...g6, but then 2. Bxd8 wins the queen.

Lesson 10

Sacrifices

A sacrifice means giving up material on purpose to get something better: a mating attack, an exposed king or a winning position.

Sacrifice or blunder?
A blunder loses material by accident, with nothing in return. A sacrifice is planned, and you've worked out what you get back.
Gambit
A sacrifice in the opening, usually a pawn, to get faster development or open lines (King's Gambit, Evans Gambit). The Queen's Gambit is named like one, but White can normally win the pawn back.
Exchange (trade)
Swapping pieces of equal value, like bishop for bishop.
Winning or losing material
Swapping pieces of different value. Remember the values: pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9.

When your opponent sacrifices

  • Calculate: is it safe to take? If you're not sure, ask what happens after you take.
  • You don't have to accept. Declining is often the safest choice.
  • Defend calmly and look for your opponent's threats.
  • Giving material back to stop an attack is often the best defence.

The Greek Gift

White sacrifices the bishop on h7 (Bxh7+), then follows with Ng5+ and Qh5. It usually works when:

  • White has a bishop on d3 aiming at h7, a knight that can reach g5 and a queen that can reach h5.
  • Black has no knight on f6 to defend h7. A white pawn on e5 often chased it away.
  • If Black can capture on g5, White needs a way to recapture or open the h-file, such as a pawn on h4.

Puzzle: the Greek Gift

White to move. All the conditions are there. Play the sacrifice and work out the follow-up.

Show answer

1. Bxh7+! Kxh7 2. Ng5+ and now:

  • 2...Kg8 3. Qh5 threatens Qh7#, and Black can't defend.
  • 2...Bxg5 3. hxg5+ opens the h-file for the rook: 3...Kg8 4. Qh5 with mate coming.

This is why the h4 pawn matters. In the same position without the h4 pawn, the sacrifice fails, because Black can take on g5 without opening the h-file.

The Fishing Pole

A trap for Black: the knight goes to g4 as "bait", and if White's h-pawn takes it, Black's h-pawn recaptures and the h-file opens straight at White's king.

Puzzle: should White take the knight?

White to move. Black's knight on g4 is attacked by the h3 pawn, and Black has just played ...h5. Is hxg4 a good idea?

Show answer

No! After 6. hxg4? hxg4 the h-file is open and the knight on f3 is attacked. If it moves, for example 7. Nxe5, then 7...Qh4 and mate follows on h2 or h1. White should leave the knight alone: 6. c3 or 6. d4 are good moves.

Lesson 11

Checkmate patterns

Checkmate means the king is in check and has no way out. Before you decide a position is mate, check all three escapes: can the king move, can the check be blocked, can the checking piece be captured?

The fastest mates

  • Fool's Mate (2 moves): 1. f3 e5 2. g4?? Qh4#. White weakened the diagonal to the king. Don't push your f- and g-pawns early.
  • Scholar's Mate (4 moves): 1. e4 e5 2. Bc4 Nc6 3. Qh5 Nf6?? 4. Qxf7#. f7 (f2 for White) is the weakest square at the start, because only the king defends it. To stop it, play 3...g6 or 3...Qe7, then develop with tempo by attacking the queen.

Puzzle: the Opera Game (Morphy, Paris 1858)

Paul Morphy played this game in a box at the Paris Opera. White to move and mate in two.

Show answer

16. Qb8+!! Nxb8 17. Rd8# The queen sacrifice drags the knight away from d7. The rook mates on the back rank, protected by the bishop on g5. This pattern is called the Opera mate.

Mating the lone king

Practise these against a friend or the Endgame Trainer until you can do them without thinking:

  • King + two rooks: the ladder. The rooks take turns pushing the king back one rank at a time.
  • King + queen: use the queen to shrink the box (a knight's move away from the king works well), then bring your king up to help.
  • King + rook: use the rook to cut the king off, bring your king opposite the enemy king, then check.
Watch out for stalemate

When the enemy king only has one or two squares left, check that it still has a legal move before you play a quiet move.

Lesson 12

Stalemate, perpetual check and draws

Stalemate: the side to move is not in check but has no legal moves. Stalemate is a draw, not a win!

Ways a game can be drawn

  • Stalemate.
  • Perpetual check: one side checks forever and the other can't escape. In the rules, this ends as a draw by repetition.
  • Threefold repetition: the same position appears three times, with the same player to move. You must claim it.
  • Fifty-move rule: 50 moves each with no capture and no pawn move. You must claim it.
  • Insufficient material: neither side can possibly mate. For example king against king, or king and bishop against king, or king and knight against king.
  • Agreement: both players agree to a draw.

Common stalemate mistakes

  • Moving the queen a knight's move away from a king in the corner.
  • Forgetting the opponent has no pawn or piece moves left.
  • Grabbing material instead of finishing the mate.
When you're losing

Look for stalemate tricks: get your king into a corner with no moves, then try to give away your last pieces. And look for perpetual check with your queen.

Puzzle: escape with a draw

White to move. Black is a queen and rook up and threatens mate. How can White save the game?

Show answer

Perpetual check. 1. Qe8+ Kh7 2. Qh5+ Kg8 3. Qe8+ and so on. The black king can only shuttle between g8 and h7, and Black can't block the checks. After the position repeats three times, White claims a draw.

Lesson 13

Endgame skills

When most pieces are gone, the king becomes a fighting piece, passed pawns become runners, and one tempo can decide the game.

Golden rules

  • Activate your king. Bring it to the centre and towards the pawns.
  • Passed pawns must be pushed, but only when it's safe. Escort them with your king.
  • In king and pawn endings, the king goes in front of the pawn.
  • In rook endings, the rook goes behind the passed pawn, whether it's yours or your opponent's.
  • Count before you push in pawn races.
  • Rook's pawns (a- and h-pawns) are often drawn, because the defending king can hide in the corner.

Key ideas

Key squares
If your king reaches a key square, the pawn will promote whatever the opponent does. For a pawn on the 2nd, 3rd or 4th rank, the key squares are the three squares two ranks in front of it (pawn on e4 → d6, e6, f6). For a pawn on the 5th or 6th rank, they are the three squares one rank in front and two ranks in front.
Opposition
Kings facing each other with one square between them. Whoever has to move must give way, so the side not to move "has the opposition". Kings 3 or 5 squares apart on the same line are in distant opposition.
Shouldering
Using your king like a bodyguard to push the enemy king away from the action.
Rule of the square
Draw an imaginary square from the pawn to its promotion square. If the defending king can step inside it, it catches the pawn.
Spare pawn move (tempo)
A pawn move that "passes", so your opponent has to move and give up the opposition.

Puzzle 1: key squares

White to move. Can White win? Would it change if Black moved first?

Show answer

White wins either way. The pawn is on e2, so its key squares are d4, e4 and f4, and White's king is already on one. With White to move, the spare move 1. e3! hands the move to Black, who has to give way: 1...Kd6 2. Kf5 and the king leads the pawn home.

Puzzle 2: opposition

Can White win? Work out the answer with White to move, then with Black to move.

Show answer

It depends who moves! The key squares are b6, c6 and d6.

White to move: draw. Black keeps the opposition: 1. Kb5 Kb7 or 1. Kd5 Kd7.

Black to move: White wins. Black has to give way, for example 1...Kd7 2. Kb6! and the white king reaches a key square.

Puzzle 3: the rule of the square

Can Black's king catch the a-pawn? Try it with White to move, then with Black to move.

Show answer

White to move: 1. a5! The new square runs a5–d5–d8–a8, and the black king on f5 can't step into it. The pawn queens.

Black to move: 1...Ke5 (or ...Ke4 or ...Ke6) steps into the square a4–e4–e8–a8, and the king catches the pawn. Draw.

Puzzle 4: the rook's pawn

White to move. White has an extra pawn and the better king. Can White win?

Show answer

No, it's a draw. Black's king just shuffles between g8 and h8. If White pushes on, for example 1. Kh6 Kg8 2. Kg6 Kh8 3. h6 Kg8 4. h7+ Kh8 5. Kh6, Black is stalemated. A defending king in the corner can't be driven out by a king and rook's pawn.

Rook endings: two positions everyone must know

The Lucena position: how to win

White to move. White's king is stuck in front of the pawn. How does White escape the checks and promote?

Show answer

Build a bridge. 1. Rd1+ Ke7 2. Rd4! puts the rook on the 4th rank. Then the king walks out: 2...Ra1 3. Kc7 Rc1+ 4. Kb6 Rb1+ 5. Kc6 Rc1+ 6. Kb5 Rb1+ 7. Rb4. The rook blocks the checks like a bridge and the pawn promotes.

The Philidor position: how to draw

Black to move. Black is a pawn down. How does Black hold the draw?

Show answer

Keep the rook on the 6th rank (your third rank), for example 1...Rb6, so White's king can't come forward. When the pawn moves to d6, the white king has lost its shelter, so switch the rook to the back and check from behind: ...Rb1 and keep checking.

Breakthrough pattern

With white pawns on f5, g5, h5 against black pawns on f7, g7, h7 (kings far away), White to move wins with 1. g6! If 1...fxg6 2. h6! gxh6 3. f6, and if 1...hxg6 2. f6! gxf6 3. h6. It's the same trick as the queenside breakthrough in Lesson 3.

Lesson 14

Your thinking routine

Strong players don't just "see" tactics. They look for them in the same order every move.

When it's your opponent's move

  • What did their last move change? What does it attack now? What did it stop defending?
  • What would they play if it were their move again?

When it's your move: checks, captures, threats

  • Checks: list every check you can give, even the silly-looking ones.
  • Captures: list every capture.
  • Threats: which moves attack something or threaten mate?
  • Look for the patterns: loose pieces, a king and queen a knight's move apart, pieces lined up on one line, a weak back rank.

Before you let go of the piece: blunder check

  • After my move, which of my pieces are unprotected?
  • What are all the checks and captures my opponent can play in reply?
Daily habit

Do 10 puzzles a day on Lichess or in Checkmate Academy. A little every day beats a lot once a week. Speed comes from pattern recognition.
