The Scotch Game

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 — the Scotch. You open the centre on move three instead of manoeuvring for twenty moves as in the Ruy Lopez or the Italian. After 3...exd4 4.Nxd4 you have a knight in the middle of the board, free development for both bishops and far less theory to learn.

  • ECO C44–C45
  • For White
  • Club level
  • 5 Black replies covered

Overview

Key ideas

  • 3.d4 exd4 4.Nxd4 gives you a pawn on e4 against a pawn on d6 or d7, so you have more space in the centre.
  • Black gets quick development in return and will attack the d4 knight or the e4 pawn straight away. Each main reply does one or the other.
  • Your c1 bishop usually goes to e3 to support the knight, and the knight on b1 heads for c3 or d2.

Typical plans

  • Against 4...Bc5: Be3, c3 and Bc4 build a solid centre, then you castle and play f3 or f4.
  • Against 4...Nf6: Nxc6 and e5. The pawn on e5 cramps Black and the game becomes a fight over that pawn.
  • Against a kingside fianchetto: castle long and attack with f3, g4 and h4.

Pawn structures

  • e4 against d6: the usual Scotch centre. You have the d5 square and more room; Black aims for ...d5 or ...f5.
  • Pawn on e5 (Mieses line): Black has doubled c-pawns and a bishop stuck on a6. Keep e5 defended and finish development.
  • Opposite-side castling (after 4...g6): speed matters more than pawns.

Why the Scotch?

It is direct and easy to understand: open lines, quick development and clear targets. Kasparov used it at world championship level, and it takes Black out of the Ruy Lopez and Italian positions they have prepared for.

If they play something odd

After 4...Nxd4 recapture with the queen: 5.Qxd4 gives you a strong centralised queen that the missing knight can no longer chase. After 4...Qf6 play 5.Be3, which usually transposes to the 4...Bc5 line.
