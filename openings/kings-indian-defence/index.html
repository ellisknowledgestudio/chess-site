The King's Indian Defence

1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 Bg7 4.e4 d6. Black lets White build a big pawn centre, then hits it with ...e5 or ...c5 and attacks. It is the choice of players who want to win with Black, from Fischer and Kasparov down to club level.

  • ECO E60–E99
  • For Black
  • Club level
  • 6 White systems covered

Overview

Key ideas

  • Develop first: ...Nf6, ...g6, ...Bg7 and castle. You can play these moves against almost anything.
  • Then strike at the centre, usually with ...e5. If White closes it with d5, the pawn chains tell each side where to play.
  • With pawns on d5 and e4 against d6 and e5, you attack on the kingside with ...f5 and White attacks on the queenside.

Typical plans

  • The kingside pawn storm: knight away from f6, then ...f5, ...f4, ...g5 and a piece attack behind the pawns.
  • Against set-ups with f4 or Bg5, switch to ...c5 and play a Benoni-style game on the queenside.
  • Keep your light-squared bishop. It is the piece that breaks through on h3 at the end of the attack.

Pawn structures

  • Closed centre (d5 and e4 v d6 and e5): a race. Your attack is aimed at the king, so it tends to be worth more.
  • Benoni structure (after ...c5 and d5): you have a queenside majority and the e-file; push ...b5.
  • Exchange on e5: the queens come off and the position is level. The d4 square is yours, the d5 square is White's.

Why the King's Indian?

You get the same set-up against 1.d4, 1.c4 and 1.Nf3, so there is less to learn than it first appears. The positions are unbalanced, and a well-prepared club player with Black often has the easier game because the attack plays itself.

If they play something odd

Against quiet systems such as the London or an early e3, play the same first moves, castle, and choose between ...e5 and ...c5 once you see where White's pieces are.
