The Grünfeld Defence

1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 d5. Black strikes at the centre at once. After the exchange on d5 White gets a broad pawn centre and Black spends the rest of the game attacking it with the g7 bishop, ...c5 and ...Nc6. It is sharp, sound and played at every level.

  • ECO D70–D99
  • For Black
  • Club level
  • 6 White systems covered

Overview

Key ideas

  • After 4.cxd5 Nxd5 5.e4 Nxc3 6.bxc3 White owns the centre. Your job is to prove it is a target, not a strength.
  • The bishop on g7, the pawn on c5 and a knight on c6 all aim at d4. A queen on a5 or a bishop on g4 adds more pressure.
  • You nearly always play ...c5. It is the move that makes the opening work.

Typical plans

  • Pile up on d4: ...Bg7, ...c5, ...Nc6, and often ...Bg4 to pin or remove a defender.
  • Exchange on d4 at the right moment to open the c-file, then use it with a rook.
  • In endgames your queenside majority (two pawns against one after ...cxd4) can produce a passed pawn.

Pawn structures

  • White centre on c3, d4, e4: strong if it advances safely, weak if it is fixed and attacked. Do not let d5 come with tempo.
  • After ...cxd4 cxd4: White has a passed d-pawn in waiting, you have the queenside majority.
  • Symmetrical (after quiet lines): no problems for Black, and the g7 bishop is the best minor piece on the board.

Why the Grünfeld?

It gives Black active piece play from the first moves instead of a cramped defence. It shares its first two moves with the King's Indian, so you can learn both and choose at move three.

If they play something odd

If White plays Nf3 before Nc3, or delays c4, you can still play ...g6, ...Bg7 and ...d5. Against 3.f3 you can play 3...d5 anyway, or switch to a King's Indian set-up with ...d6.
